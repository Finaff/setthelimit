## 0. TED talk — Can we build AI without losing control over it? (June 2016)
https://www.ted.com/talks/sam_harris_can_we_build_ai_without_losing_control_over_it/transcript

- "One of the things that worries me most about the development of AI at this point is that we seem unable to marshal an appropriate emotional response to the dangers that lie ahead. I am unable to marshal this response, and I'm giving this talk."
- "The only alternative, and this is what lies behind door number two, is that we continue to improve our intelligent machines year after year after year. At a certain point, we will build machines that are smarter than we are, and once we have machines that are smarter than we are, they will begin to improve themselves. And then we risk what the mathematician IJ Good called an 'intelligence explosion,' that the process could get away from us."
- "We want to cure diseases like Alzheimer's and cancer. We want to understand economic systems. We want to improve our climate science. So we will do this, if we can. The train is already out of the station, and there's no brake to pull."
- "The concern is really that we will build machines that are so much more competent than we are that the slightest divergence between their goals and our own could destroy us. Just think about how we relate to ants. […] whenever their presence seriously conflicts with one of our goals […] we annihilate them without a qualm."
- "Electronic circuits function about a million times faster than biochemical ones […] So you set it running for a week, and it will perform 20,000 years of human-level intellectual work, week after week after week. How could we even understand, much less constrain, a mind making this sort of progress?"
- "A few trillionaires could grace the covers of our business magazines while the rest of the world would be free to starve. And what would the Russians or the Chinese do if they heard that some company in Silicon Valley was about to deploy a superintelligent AI? […] This is a winner-take-all scenario."
- "I think we need something like a Manhattan Project on the topic of artificial intelligence. Not to build it, because I think we'll inevitably do that, but to understand how to avoid an arms race and to build it in a way that is aligned with our interests."
- "We have no idea how long it will take us to create the conditions to do that safely. […] Fifty years is not that much time to meet one of the greatest challenges our species will ever face."

## Where he is uncertain

Harris argues from first principles about intelligence (speed, unpredictability, instrumental goals) and is confident on the danger axis: alignment is "where we ruin everything", control is unlikely once the system is smarter, and safety is not keeping pace. He is far less specific on the speed axis. He wants "some form of regulation" but admits he is "not quite sure what that could look like"; he never discusses licences, compute thresholds, inspections or open weights in his own voice, so s4, s6 and s11 are inferences. On the race he is openly torn: in the same 2026 conversation he says "we just cannot lose to China" and asks "How do we become slow and careful under those conditions?", then, of the social-media race the US won, "What exactly did we win?". He has never called for a halt (TED: "no brake to pull"; 2023: "not everyone's going to stop"), and signed the 2023 extinction-risk statement but neither the pause letter nor the 2025 superintelligence prohibition, so s1 and s13 rest on what he declines to say as much as on what he says. He gives no probability of catastrophe of his own (d1 is inferred from his endorsement of the "doomer case" and his treatment of 10–30% as alarming) and no timeline for automation (p2).
