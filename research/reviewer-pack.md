# Set the Limit — reviewer pack (propositions v4, 19 Sept 2026)

Thank you for reading this. Set the Limit (setthelimit.com) is a short public test on the debate about slowing
down AI. People rate 16 propositions from 0 to 100 and get a position on two axes: **road conditions** (how
dangerous they think advanced AI is) and **speed** (what pace they would set). Sixteen public figures are placed
on the same map from their public statements, and each can replace our predictions with their own answers.

The site only works if people on *both* sides find the propositions fair. That is what we are asking you to judge.

## What we ask (about 20 minutes)

1. For each proposition, a mark from 1 (loaded, would not answer it as written) to 5 (fair, I would answer it
   as written), and a word on anything that tilts it: a loaded adjective, a hidden second claim, a false
   choice, a strawman in either steelman.
2. Your own 16 answers, if you are willing. They let us check where you land, and you can ask for them to be
   used as your dot on the map, labelled as yours.
3. Anything you think the test misses that matters to *your* side, and anything you think it should drop.

Rules the propositions were written to: one claim per sentence; plain words; no adjective that takes a side;
equal numbers phrased in each direction on each axis, at equal weight, so agreeing with everything or with nothing
lands you in the middle; two steelmen per item, each written to satisfy the side it represents. Two adversarial
reviews and one independent audit of the figure predictions have already been applied (research/review-v1.md,
review-v2.md, audit-v1.md).

## The propositions, as mirrored pairs

*Type* prob = the slider reads Very unlikely ↔ Very likely; agree = Strongly disagree ↔ Strongly agree.
*Dir* + means agreeing moves you toward black ice (danger axis) or no limit (speed axis); − the reverse.

### d1 · danger · agree · dir + · weight 1.5

**There is at least a 1-in-10 chance that, this century, advanced AI causes a catastrophe humanity never recovers from.**

*In plain words:* The headline question. Not 'will AI cause problems' but 'could it end us, or end our say over our own future for good'. 'Never recovers from' means extinction, or permanently losing control of our future. One in ten is the bar: not certain, not negligible. If your own guess is right around one in ten, the middle of the slider is your answer.

*Why people agree:* We are building minds that may outthink us, we can't yet verify what they want, and we only get one try. Several of the people building these systems put their own estimate in this range or higher; 10% is a conservative reading.

*Why people disagree:* AI is software: it has no more wish to take over than a calculator does. Every previous 'end of the world' technology story was wrong, and a number like 10% is a guess dressed up as a probability.

### d6 · danger · agree · dir − · weight 1

**Over the next ten years, the serious harms from AI will be familiar ones like fraud, surveillance and lost jobs, not catastrophes.**

*In plain words:* Where's the real damage: in the everyday harms already happening, or in a disaster still ahead? 'Catastrophe' here means something on the scale of a pandemic or a war, or a loss of human control, not a bad decade for one profession.

*Why people agree:* Fraud, deepfakes, biased decisions and displaced workers are here now, hurting real people, and they are problems we already know how to police. Talk of machines taking over pulls attention and money away from the harms we can actually see and fix.

*Why people disagree:* Both can be true, and the second kind can't be undone. Preparing for the harm you can't reverse is not a distraction from the ones you can, and ten years is not a long time in this field.

### d3 · danger · agree · dir + · weight 1

**At some point, AI ability will probably jump so suddenly that nobody has time to respond.**

*In plain words:* The 'takeoff' question. Does ability climb step by step, with time to adjust, or could it leap in one go, so that nobody has time to react?

*Why people agree:* AI already helps build AI. Once systems improve the tools that improve them, progress can compound in months, not decades, and governments and companies move in years.

*Why people disagree:* Progress has been steady and visible for a decade, limited by data, chips and money. Each generation takes years and billions; a machine that suddenly makes itself vastly smarter is a thought experiment, not something anyone has seen.

### d9 · danger · agree · dir − · weight 1.5

**As AI becomes more capable, our ability to keep it safe will keep pace.**

*In plain words:* Today's models are taught to follow instructions and refuse harmful requests, mostly by people rating their answers. On the path we're on, will safety methods improve as fast as the AI does, even once the student is smarter than the teachers?

*Why people agree:* Each generation so far has been easier to steer than the last, not harder. Smarter models understand instructions better, and we get to use the previous model to help check the next one.

*Why people disagree:* Training by human feedback works while humans can tell good answers from bad. Once the system is smarter than its graders, it can produce answers that look right rather than are right, and we would have no way to tell the difference.

---

### d5 · danger · agree · dir + · weight 1.5

**If we build AI smarter than us, it will most likely end up with goals we didn't choose for it.**

*In plain words:* Not 'AI will hate us' but 'we won't manage to give it the goals we meant'. Would a smarter-than-human system end up wanting something other than what we intended?

*Why people agree:* We don't know how to spell out human values precisely, and training rewards what we can measure, not what we mean. A smarter system finds the gaps between the two.

*Why people disagree:* Current systems are trained on human writing and human feedback and come out broadly helpful. Being smart doesn't make you want power; there is no evidence that intelligence brings alien goals with it. That's a story, not a finding.

### d7 · danger · agree · dir − · weight 1.5

**If advanced AI turns out to be dangerous, humans will still be able to keep it under control.**

*In plain words:* We've built things that could kill millions, like nuclear weapons and engineered viruses, and so far kept the lid on. If AI proved dangerous, could people, companies and governments rein it in too?

*Why people agree:* We have kept the lid on nuclear weapons and engineered viruses for eighty years. AI is powerful, but it is still made and run by people and companies who can be watched, sued and shut down, and the defenders will have powerful AI on their side too.

*Why people disagree:* Nuclear weapons don't improve themselves, copy themselves or make decisions. Institutions move in years and this may move in months. And 'so far' includes several near-misses that came down to luck.

---

### s1 · speed · agree · dir − · weight 1.5

**All work toward AI smarter than humans should stop until we know how to make it safe, even if that takes decades.**

*In plain words:* The full-stop position: not slower, not more careful, but a halt on the road to superhuman AI until safety is solved.

*Why people agree:* You don't run an experiment that could kill everyone before you know how to run it safely. Decades of delay cost something; extinction costs everything.

*Why people disagree:* A halt can't be verified, would be ignored by the least careful actors, and forfeits every benefit while solving nothing, because you learn how to make a thing safe by building it.

### s13 · speed · agree · dir + · weight 1.5

**There should be no deliberate slowing down of AI development.**

*In plain words:* The no-limit position, the mirror of the full stop: not 'careful and fast' but 'as fast as we can'.

*Why people agree:* Every year of delay is a year of disease, poverty and drudgery we could have ended. Progress has always come with fears, and the fears have always been wrong. The way to get safety is to build, learn and fix.

*Why people disagree:* 'As fast as we can' means no time to notice a mistake before it's made at scale. Speed is a choice, and choosing it for a technology we don't yet understand is choosing not to look.

---

### s4 · speed · agree · dir − · weight 1

**The most powerful AI projects should be limited by an international treaty with inspections, as nuclear weapons are.**

*In plain words:* A global agreement with inspectors, so that no country or company can quietly race ahead.

*Why people agree:* The biggest AI projects need enormous clusters of specialised chips that can be counted and monitored, the way uranium is. If the danger is global, the answer has to be too.

*Why people disagree:* Treaties take decades, the rivals don't trust each other, and a limit on computing power is a limit on a moving target. It would be broken quietly, enforced selectively, and hand the lead to whoever cheats.

### s5 · speed · agree · dir + · weight 1

**As long as rival countries keep pushing ahead in AI, your own country should too.**

*In plain words:* The geopolitics argument. Even if you'd like everyone to slow down, should your own country hold back while others don't? If your country is not a major AI builder, answer for the group of countries it stands with.

*Why people agree:* AI leadership is economic and military leadership. Slowing down while rivals accelerate is not safety; it's handing the decision to people who care less about it than you do.

*Why people disagree:* The 'race' is mostly a story the racers tell: capabilities spread in months anyway, and being first to a system nobody can control is not a prize. If the danger is real, the answer is a deal that binds everyone, not a sprint.

---

### s6 · speed · agree · dir − · weight 1

**Before a powerful AI model is released, its makers should have to show a regulator that it is safe.**

*In plain words:* Who carries the burden of proof: the builder, as with new medicines, or the regulator, as with most software? Agree means no release until the builder has shown a public body that the model is safe.

*Why people agree:* When a product can cause harm at scale, the maker proves safety first. That's how aviation and pharmaceuticals earned public trust.

*Why people disagree:* 'Prove it's safe' has no end point for a general technology. It becomes a veto held by whoever writes the test, and small players can't afford to pass it.

### s11 · speed · agree · dir + · weight 1

**Developers should be free to release even the most powerful AI models for anyone to download, run and modify.**

*In plain words:* 'Open weights' means the model itself is published, not just access to it through a company. Should that stay legal even for the most powerful models? Good for science and competition, or a gift to bad actors?

*Why people agree:* Openness is how we found and fixed the flaws in every other technology. Concentrating the strongest models in a few hands is the real danger.

*Why people disagree:* You can't recall an open model. Once it's out, every safeguard can be stripped by anyone, and the next generation may be capable of real damage.

---

### s8 · speed · agree · dir − · weight 1.5

**Lowering the chance of catastrophe would be worth delaying AI, and its benefits, by a few years.**

*In plain words:* The trade-off in one sentence. Delay has a price: cures, growth and discoveries that arrive later. Is a lower chance of the worst outcome worth paying it?

*Why people agree:* A few years is nothing against the whole future. If waiting buys safety, it's the cheapest insurance ever offered.

*Why people disagree:* 'A few years' is millions of preventable deaths from diseases we could have cured sooner, and there is no guarantee the delay buys anything except a head start for whoever didn't wait.

### s10 · speed · agree · dir + · weight 1.5

**The lives that faster AI progress could save, through earlier cures and discoveries, outweigh the case for slowing down.**

*In plain words:* The cost of caution. If AI would have found cures sooner, delay isn't free: people die in the meantime who otherwise wouldn't. Does that settle the question?

*Why people agree:* AI is already speeding up drug discovery and diagnosis. Ten years of that, multiplied across the world's sick, is a body count we never see, because the people who would have been saved die quietly of the same old diseases.

*Why people disagree:* Medical progress is limited by trials, regulation and biology, not by a shortage of clever software, so the lives-saved figure is a projection, not a fact. And no cure helps anyone if the same technology ends us.

---

### p2 · profile (horizon; not on either axis) · prob

**Within 20 years, AI will do most of the thinking work that humans are paid for today.**

*In plain words:* How big and how soon? This is about capability, not danger.

*Why people agree:* In five years we went from autocomplete to systems that write code, pass professional exams and carry out multi-step tasks on their own. Twenty more years of that is hard to imagine ending anywhere else.

*Why people disagree:* Demos are not jobs. Most work involves bodies, trust, context and responsibility, and every automation wave has taken longer and gone less far than promised.

### p4 · profile (concentration; not on either axis) · agree

**A few companies or governments controlling the most powerful AI is a bigger danger than AI acting on its own.**

*In plain words:* Which do you fear more: the machine, or the people who own it?

*Why people agree:* Every past technology's harms came from who controlled it. Superhuman AI in a few hands is superhuman power in a few hands.

*Why people disagree:* Concentration is a familiar problem with familiar remedies. A system that no one controls, including its owners, is a new one.
